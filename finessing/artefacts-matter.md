# Artefacts Matter

The visual elements of your thesisfigures, tables, diagrams, and code snippetsare not decorative additions. They are integral components of your argument and communication strategy. Poor quality artefacts can undermine even the strongest technical content.

## The Problem

Students often treat visual elements as afterthoughts, leading to:
- Pixelated screenshots that obscure important details
- Tables with inconsistent formatting and unclear headers
- Figures without proper captions or context
- Code snippets that are poorly formatted or incomplete
- Charts with illegible axes labels or confusing color schemes

These issues force readers to work harder to understand your content, breaking the flow of comprehension.

## The Solution

**Invest time in creating professional-quality artefacts.** Every figure, table, and code block should be crafted with the same care as your prose.

### Practical Steps

1. **Use vector graphics** when possiblethey scale cleanly and remain crisp at any size
2. **Make screenshots deliberate**crop tightly, ensure high resolution, and highlight key areas
3. **Design consistent tables**use clear headers, consistent alignment, and appropriate spacing
4. **Caption everything meaningfully**each artefact should be understandable without referring to the main text
5. **Test for accessibility**ensure color choices work for colorblind readers and text remains legible when printed in grayscale

### Example

Instead of:
> See Figure 3 for the results.

Write:
> Figure 3 demonstrates that response times decreased by 40% after implementing the caching layer, with the most significant improvements occurring during peak traffic periods.

## Why This Works

Quality artefacts serve multiple functions: they provide evidence for your claims, break up dense text for better readability, and demonstrate your attention to detail and professionalism. When your visual elements are polished and purposeful, they enhance rather than detract from your argument.

Remember: your thesis may be the first impression a future employer or collaborator has of your technical communication skills. Make it count.
